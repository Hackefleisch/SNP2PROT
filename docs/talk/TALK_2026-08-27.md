## The script

### Slide 1 — The question (0:35)

> Transcription factors bind DNA, and which sites they bind decides which genes turn on. The
> question we care about is a step past that: **when a single amino acid in the binding domain
> changes, does what it binds change too?** That is the question behind every regulatory variant
> in a patient genome.
>
> So we need a training set that varies on **both axes at once** — many DNA sites per protein,
> *and* many single-residue variants per protein. That second half is what almost no dataset has,
> and it is what the last months went into building.

### Slide 2 — What we built (0:50)

> This is the dataset. Nineteen published protein-binding-microarray studies, all the same assay,
> re-parsed from raw files into one table. **1,338 DNA-binding domains, each measured against the
> same 32,896 eight-base sites — 44 million measurements.** 56 protein families, 131 organisms.
>
> *(left panel)* Every protein against every site — the blocks are related proteins binding
> related motifs. *(right)* Only 0.2% of pairs bind. That is not a bug to fix, it is what the
> assay says: a transcription factor binds a small minority of sites, and every negative here was
> **measured** and came back below threshold, not merely absent.
>
> *(switch to the UMAP)* And this is the protein side. Each dot is one domain. Panel A: the
> families — homeodomain is the thick one, 428 domains. Panel C is the part I want you to notice:
> **173 single-residue variants sitting on top of their own wild type**, in 108 tight clusters.
> That is the unusual thing in this dataset and the only reason our question is askable.
>
> Panel B is the caveat, and it will come back in two slides. It colours each domain by how
> similar its closest relative in the set is. Most of the map is pale — **1,057 of our 1,165
> clusters have exactly one member.** The panel is broad and shallow.

### Slide 3 — The model, and the space it builds (1:05)

> The model is deliberately simple. Two encoders, one shared space.
>
> The protein goes through a **protein language model** — a network pre-trained on millions of
> protein sequences — which turns the domain into one vector. That model is **frozen**: we never
> train it, we read its output from a cache. The DNA site goes through a small convolutional
> network, run on both strands and averaged, because the array measures both strands together.
>
> Both land in the same 256-dimensional space, and the **score of a protein–site pair is just
> the cosine of the angle between them.** Training pulls binding pairs together and pushes
> non-binding pairs apart — this is the same idea as CLIP, which matches images to captions.
>
> The whole trainable model is under half a million parameters. One run takes three minutes;
> the entire experiment grid — 38 runs — took under two hours on one desktop GPU.
>
> *(switch to the DNA map)* And this is what the model does with it. Every faint dot is one of
> the 32,896 DNA sites, laid out so that sites the model thinks are similar sit together. The
> blue dots are the sites this particular protein **actually binds** — measured, on the array.
> And the red ring is where the **model** puts the protein: on the single site it scores
> highest.
>
> For CREB the ring lands right inside the blue cluster. Same for Tbx5. And Lhx5, third panel,
> is a protein this model **never saw in training** — the ring still lands on a site it really
> binds. **Across all 1,338 proteins, the model's single top-scoring site is a genuine binding
> site 81% of the time.**
>
> The last panel is the DNA landscape on its own, coloured by how many proteins bind each site.
> **19,065 of the 32,896 sites are bound by nothing at all**, and the busiest one is bound by 238
> different proteins.

### Slide 4 — The bar (0:50)

> Now the part I care most about you agreeing with, because everything else depends on it.
>
> There is already a standard way to guess what an uncharacterised transcription factor binds:
> **find the most sequence-similar TF you already know, and copy its motif.** That is essentially
> how CIS-BP does inference. So that is our baseline — and it is not a strawman, it is the
> incumbent method, and it is the first thing anyone in this room would ask about.
>
> We tested five different ways of holding proteins back — random proteins, groups of related
> proteins, a whole family removed, and two that remove mutations specifically. Orange is
> copy-the-nearest-neighbour, blue is our model.
>
> **The model beats it on three runs out of thirty-eight.** I want to be straightforward about
> that. It is not the result we hoped for, and the honest headline for today is what we learned
> from *why*.

### Slide 5 — What we found (1:00)

> This is the finding. We took every held-out protein from every run, and sorted them by one
> thing only: **how similar their closest training protein was.**
>
> On the right, where a held-out protein has a 90%-identical relative in training, both methods
> score around 0.9 — but so would anything, because copying the relative is already almost right.
> On the left, where the closest relative is under 30% identical, **both methods collapse to
> 0.03.** Neither knows anything.
>
> So the performance curve everyone plots for these models is not really about the model. It is
> **one variable: how close the nearest known protein is.** That is the most useful thing this
> round produced, and it is a claim about the whole field's benchmarks, not just ours.
>
> We then went looking for what is actually limiting us, and ruled two things out by measurement:
> the shared space is not too small — mathematically it can represent 0.92 and we deliver 0.28 —
> and the protein encoder is not too small either; it is already extracting everything a linear
> map of that vector can extract. What is left is either the **protein representation itself** —
> we squash a whole domain into one average vector, and a one-residue change is under 3% of that
> vector's scale — or simply **not enough related proteins to learn from.** The single run we won
> is the single run that had 155 same-family proteins in training, two and a half times any other.

### Slide 6 — Where this goes (0:40)

> Three things next, and two of them need you.
>
> **One — a second assay.** The Codebook SELEX data gives us a second, independent measurement
> and a much larger human panel, and it lets us do the experiment that actually convinces people:
> train on microarray data, test on SELEX.
>
> **Two — structure.** Right now the model sees a protein as one averaged vector of its whole
> sequence. A predicted structure knows which residues actually face the DNA. That is the
> hypothesis we most want to test, and it is what the ensembles are for.
>
> **Three — the thing all of this is for.** If we can predict what a *mutated* domain binds
> without ever having seen that mutation, we can go from a variant in a genome to a changed
> binding site to a changed gene. That is the target. We are not there, but we now know exactly
> what is in the way and how we would tell if we had fixed it.

## What I want to ask you

Two of these block work; the rest are cheap for you and expensive for us.

**1. SELEX — can we lock the k-mer format now, before you generate anything?** *(blocking)*
The spec is four lines and the third one fails **silently** if it is missed:

- **k = 8**, nothing else;
- over the **same 32,896** non-redundant 8-mers our tables use;
- canonicalised as the **lexicographically smaller** of `(s, revcomp(s))`;
- a **continuous** score per 8-mer, exhaustive — every 8-mer scored, positives and negatives.

If the canonicalisation runs the other way, the table validates, looks the right size, and joins
on almost nothing. Also worth agreeing: what timeline, and do we get per-8-mer read coverage so
we can judge how much to trust the negatives?

**2. Structures — two decisions before the run.** *(blocking)*
- **A fixed ensemble size `N` for every domain.** We represent an ensemble by its *spread*, and a
  standard deviation from 10 structures is not the same object as one from 50. Mixed sizes used
  as-is is the one option that is actually wrong.
- **All 1,338 domains, or just the 281 in variant-bearing clusters?** The subset is ~21% of the
  work and is where an ensemble earns its cost. But then the sequence-vs-structure comparison has
  to be run on that subset for *every* arm, and the headline number becomes a subset result. That
  is a decision to take together, not around each other.
- Also: we need the **per-structure embeddings**, not only the structures, and a common
  superposition.

**3. Which residues actually contact the DNA?** *(this is the biology question I most want)*
Our next cheap experiment is to average the protein embedding over the **DNA-contacting residues
only**, instead of over all ~77. For homeodomain we can use the recognition helix. But there are
56 families in the set. If you can give me the recognition positions for **forkhead** and one or
two others, that alone covers most of our variants — homeodomain and forkhead carry 97 of the 173.

**4. Do you know of any other panel with designed single-residue variants?**
This is our real bottleneck: 84 of our 173 variants are homeodomain, and the whole question is
whether mutation sensitivity *transfers across folds*. Any published assay that mutated one
domain systematically — especially outside homeodomain — is worth more to us than more proteins.

**5. A sanity check on the noise floor.** For the 47 proteins measured by two different labs on
the *same* assay, the median pair agrees on only **45.8%** of the 8-mers either one called
binding. Within one lab, replicates agree 70–72%. Does that match your experience? We are using
45.8% as the ceiling any model should be read against, and I would rather have it challenged now.

**6. One unexplained result, in case it rings a bell.** The DNA-binding-protein-adapted language
model (ESM-DBP) doesn't just fail when every homeodomain is removed from training — it goes
**anti-correlated**, 61% of held-out homeodomains scoring below chance. The generic model
(ESM-2) does not. We have no explanation.

## Formulas, in plain Unicode

Paste straight into a Google Slides text box — no equation editor needed. Set the box to a
monospace face (Roboto Mono) so the alignment holds.

**The score of a protein–site pair** — cosine similarity in the shared space, over a learned
temperature `τ`:

```
s(p, k)  =  ⟨ f(p), g(k) ⟩ / τ
```

where `f` is the protein tower, `g` the DNA tower, and both outputs are unit length.

**The training objective** — multi-positive InfoNCE. For protein `p` with binding sites `P` and
non-binding sites `N`, plus the learned "binds nothing" anchor `ν`:

```
              1                        exp s(p, k⁺)
L(p)  =  − ───────  ·  Σ      log  ──────────────────────────────
             |P|      k⁺ ∈ P        exp s(p, k⁺) + Σ  exp s(p, k)
                                                  k ∈ N ∪ {ν}
```

In words: *every site this protein binds should outrank every site it does not.* The loss hits
zero exactly when that is true, which is exactly when AUPR = 1 — the objective and the metric
agree at their optimum.

**The same thing, as it is actually computed** — one `logsumexp` per protein, not one per pair:

```
logC(p) = logsumexp  s(p, k)        over k ∈ N ∪ {ν}

L(p)    = mean  softplus( logC(p) − s(p, k⁺) )      over k⁺ ∈ P
```

**Sequence identity**, the variable slide 5 is about:

```
identity  =  1 − n_edits / aligned_length
```

**AUPR**, if anyone asks what the y-axis is: rank all 32,896 sites for one protein, and measure
how far up the ranking that protein's real binding sites land. 1.0 is perfect; **random guessing
scores that protein's own positive rate, roughly 0.002.** Averaged over proteins, never pooled —
so every protein counts once regardless of how many sites it binds.

**Why not accuracy:** at 466 negatives per positive, calling everything "does not bind" scores
99.8% accuracy and is worthless.
